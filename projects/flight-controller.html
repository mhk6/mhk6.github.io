ELRS Flight Controller

KiCad · STM32F405 · ExpressLRS · Betaflight

A four-layer flight controller for a 5-inch quadcopter, designed from a blank schematic in KiCad. The part most boards leave to a plug-in module, the 2.4 GHz ExpressLRS receiver, is on the board itself, so the radio and its antenna match are part of the design instead of something bolted on afterward.

I picked every part, worked out the values against the datasheets, and drew the whole thing myself across nine sheets. It runs Betaflight on an STM32F405, which is the constraint that shapes most of the rest of the board.

Power

The board takes pack voltage off the ESC ribbon, so it has to survive anything from 2S to 6S. Input protection, a 60 V buck down to 5 V, and two separate 3.3 V regulators, one of which exists only to give the gyro a quiet supply.

Flight loop

The STM32F405 reads the gyro over its own SPI bus and drives four DShot motor outputs off timer pins. Betaflight expects specific peripherals on specific pins, so the pin map came out of working backwards from what the firmware needs and checking each assignment against the alternate-function tables. An ICM-42688-P handles rotation and acceleration, with a DPS310 barometer and external compass pads on I2C.

Integrated ExpressLRS receiver

An SX1280 does the 2.4 GHz radio and an ESP8285 runs the ExpressLRS firmware over SPI, with CRSF back to the STM32 on a UART. Putting the receiver on the board rather than on a module means the RF match, the crystal and the antenna are part of this design. The match follows Semtech's reference, and the stack-up is signal, ground, ground, signal so the RF run keeps a solid reference plane.

Logging and setup

A 16 MB flash chip records blackbox logs on a separate SPI bus from the gyro, so logging can't interfere with the sensor the flight loop reads every cycle. Configuration and firmware go over USB-C, with an ESD array on the data pair and a divider that tells the MCU whether it's on USB or on a battery.

Everything that plugs into it

Four motor signal pads carry DShot out to the ESCs. The board commands the motors but never sources their current, which keeps the high-current path off it entirely. The rest of the edge is a header per peripheral: GPS and video transmitter on their own UARTs, a WS2812 strip behind a 5 V level shifter, a buzzer on a transistor driver, and battery sense into an ADC pin.
