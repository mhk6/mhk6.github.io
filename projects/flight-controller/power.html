Power

TPS54360 · AP2114H · AP2112K · SMBJ33CA · DMP6180SK3

The board runs off the flight pack, 2S to 6S, so anywhere from 6.6 V to 25.2 V. Everything on it needs one of three rails.

Both 3.3 V regulators hang off the 5 V rail rather than the battery. A linear regulator burns the whole difference as heat, so dropping 25 V straight to 3.3 V at 150 mA would be 3.3 W in a small package. From 5 V each one only drops 1.7 V.

01Protection

SMBJ33CA · DMP6180SK3 · BZT52C12

The order at the input is pad, bulk capacitor, TVS, then the reverse-polarity FET. The FET is last so the TVS and the bulk cap clamp and damp an incoming transient before it reaches the FET.

Unidirectional to bidirectional

I first fitted the unidirectional part, which is the normal choice for a rail that never goes negative. That changed once the ESC ribbon became a second battery entry point. The XT30 is keyed and can't be reversed, but the ESC side is a bare 2.54 mm header that can go in backwards or offset by a pin.

On a reversed pack a unidirectional TVS forward-conducts at about 0.7 V, which puts a short across the battery. The bidirectional part blocks instead, because 25.2 V is well below its 36.7 V breakdown. Both are the same datasheet row with identical ratings, so the change was one character of the part number.

How the TVS was sized, and what the swap doesn't fix

A TVS is picked on three voltages. Standoff has to sit above the highest normal rail or the part conducts on a full pack, breakdown is where it starts conducting hard, and clamping is what the node actually reaches at full surge current. A charged 6S pack is 25.2 V, so 33 V standoff leaves about 7.8 V of clearance.

clamping voltage against every part on the node VC=53.3 V<60 V<63 V<65 V IPP=600 W53.3 V=11 A FET 60 V, bulk cap 63 V, buck input 65 V. Surge current on a 10/1000 µs pulse.

The bulk electrolytic is still upstream of the FET and still polarized, so a reversed pack can damage it. Moving the bulk cap and TVS downstream of the FET would fix that, but then the FET takes the full hot-plug inrush with no bulk capacitance in front of it, and the TVS no longer clamps spikes at the connector where they arrive.

02Buck

TPS54360DDAR · 60 V · 3.5 A · 600 kHz

Strip the controller away and a buck is five parts, and the switch is doing all the work. If you didn't care about getting clean DC you could stop there: chop the input on and off at 20 percent duty and the average of those pulses is 20 percent of the input. That's the whole conversion. The problem is that the output isn't 5 V, it's 25 V a fifth of the time and 0 V the rest.

So the inductor and the capacitor exist to turn that average into something you can actually use. The inductor resists the change in current so it ramps instead of stepping, the diode gives that current a path when the switch opens, and the capacitor holds the output between pulses. Everything the datasheet asks you to size comes out of that loop.

Every value matches the board: 25 V in, 8.2 µH, 94 µF, and a 2.5 Ω load standing in for the 2 A the rail actually draws.

The duty cycle in that sim is 22.8 percent, not the 20 percent the ideal ratio asks for. The difference is the diode. During the off time the inductor doesn't see ground, it sees a diode drop below it, so volt-second balance gives Vout = D(Vin + Vd) − Vd, and the switch has to stay on longer to make up for it. That loss is also why the diode is the part a synchronous converter replaces with a second transistor.

Sizing against the datasheet's conditions gives 7.3 µH minimum, so 8.2 µH standard. The saturation rule is that the inductor should hold up to at least the IC's switch current limit, so a hard short can't saturate the core before the IC folds back. The Würth part saturates at 5.8 A against a 5.5 A nominal limit, and the 3.9 A full-load peak stays under even the 4.5 A minimum limit.

How much it has to deliver

The IC is rated 3.5 A, but the number that matters is the real load. Adding up the worst-case operating current of every chip gives 185 mA of silicon. The external loads are guesses, so they're generous and doubled.

LED strip 0.5 A, other 5 V loads 1 A, silicon 0.185 A, with 2× margin IOUT=(1+0.5+0.185)×2=3.37 A Against a 3.5 A rating. It's a sizing ceiling; in flight the 5 V rail draws about 0.7 A.

Switching frequency, turn-on threshold and output capacitors

One resistor on the RT pin sets the frequency.

RT=101756fSW1.008=161 kΩ R in kΩ, f in kHz, at 600 kHz. Fitted 162 kΩ 1%.

The turn-on divider can't be copied from the datasheet. TI's values turn on at 8 V, which would never start on a 2S pack. Turn-on needs to sit just above the buck's own dropout so the rail holds as the battery sags instead of cycling on and off in flight.

dropout floor for 5 V out is 5.56 V, so target 6.4 V on and 5.9 V off RUV1=Vstart−VstopIHYS=147 kΩ Fitted 150 kΩ and 33.2 kΩ, which back-substitute to 6.44 V on, 5.93 V off, about 0.5 V of hysteresis above the floor.

For the output capacitors three criteria apply and the largest wins. Transient response needs 29.2 µF, overshoot on load release needs 24.6 µF, and ripple needs only 7.8 µF with ESR under 27 mΩ.

03LDOs

AP2114H-3.3 · AP2112K-3.3

The digital rail feeds the MCU, flash, radio and receiver, and has to absorb the ESP's WiFi burst, so it's the 1 A part. The analog rail feeds the gyro and nothing else.

The ICM-42688-P senses Coriolis force as a small capacitance change and amplifies it heavily, so supply noise couples into the measurement and Betaflight's PID loop turns that into motor jitter. Betaflight specifies a dedicated LDO for this part, with output noise under 50 µV peak to peak and rejection above 70 dB down to around 1 Hz.

Why a regulator beats the buck here

A buck can't meet that, because its output is a filtered switching waveform and keeps some ripple at 600 kHz and its harmonics. An LDO has no switch. It's a linear pass element whose feedback loop rejects input ripple, so it takes the buck's 5 V and attenuates the ripple by its rejection ratio while adding only its own noise floor.

vgyro≈vripple×10−PSRR/20 Ripple on the 5 V rail, attenuated by the LDO's rejection.

04Battery sense

A divider scales the pack down into the ADC range, with a small capacitor filtering switching noise. Betaflight uses the reading for the OSD, the low-voltage warning, cell-count detection, sag compensation and the capacity estimate.

It started at 10 k over 1 k, dividing by 11, which puts a full 6S pack at 2.29 V. That was wrong:

  1. It was checked against 36 V. That's the TVS breakdown voltage, not the voltage the node reaches.
  2. The clamping voltage is 53.3 V. Divided by 11, that's 4.85 V at the pin.
  3. The pin's absolute maximum is 4.0 V. The surge the TVS exists to survive would have gone into the ADC.
  4. No clamp diode covers it. PC2 is a five-volt-tolerant pin, and those omit the upper diode to VDD. The tolerance also doesn't apply in analog mode, which is how the pin is used.

The divider is now 18 k over 1 k, dividing by 19. A full 6S pack reads 1.33 V and the 53.3 V clamp lands at 2.81 V, under the 4.0 V limit.
