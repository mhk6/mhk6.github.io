USB

USB-C · USB 2.0 full speed · USBLC6-2SC6 · B0530W

One USB-C port does configuration, firmware updates and bench power. The interesting parts are the handshake that makes a USB-C host turn its power on at all, and the single diode that lets the board run with no battery attached.

01What the port is for

Three jobs, and they shape everything else on the sheet.

JobWhat it needs
Configurationa serial port over USB, for the Betaflight configurator
Firmwarethe STM32's built-in DFU bootloader, entered with the boot button
Bench powerVBUS reaching the 5 V rail without a battery connected

The third one is why this sheet has a diode on it. A host port supplies well over the board's 185 mA of silicon, so the whole thing runs on USB alone for setting up and calibrating. The motors can't spin while it does, because the ESCs are off-board and on battery power, which is exactly what you want on a bench.

Debug access is separate. Firmware goes on over DFU, so the SWD header exists only for stepping through code, which isn't something you do with release firmware.

02The sink handshake

Miss these two resistors and the port is dead. The host never turns VBUS on.

USB-C doesn't just connect power to the pins. The host puts a pull-up on its configuration channel lines and waits to see them pulled down by whatever got plugged in. That pull-down is how a device says it's a sink and wants power. No pull-down, no attach detected, no VBUS.

So the port gets a 5.1 kΩ resistor from each configuration channel pin to ground. Two of them, one per pin, and they are never tied together. The pins are what let the connector work either way up, and shorting them removes the host's ability to tell which orientation it's in.

The checker that runs over this schematic has an explicit rule asserting the two are on different nets, because tying them is an easy mistake that looks tidy on a schematic.

03Data lines

USB-C carries a data pair on both sides of the connector so it works either way round, and this is a USB 2.0 device, so the two sides are simply tied together at the footprint.

Each line also has a series resistor footprint, and both are fitted with 0 Ω. Those resistors are conventional on USB designs, but ST's application note is explicit that the F405's full-speed PHY already drives at the impedance the spec asks for, so adding series resistance only makes things worse. The footprints stay because they cost nothing and give somewhere to put a real value if a board ever needs it.

04ESD

The data pair goes through a clamp array before it reaches the MCU. A USB connector is the one part of the board a person touches, and a static discharge from a hand is thousands of volts into a pin rated for 3.6.

The array sits right at the connector, so a strike is shunted to ground before it has any board to travel along. Its own supply pin ties to VBUS, which gives the clamp diodes the rail they clamp against.

05Knowing it's plugged in

The MCU needs to know a host is present, so it only powers up the USB peripheral and asserts its pull-up when there's something to talk to. VBUS is 5 V and the pin tolerates far less, so a divider scales it down.

Two constraints have to hold at once. The voltage has to stay under the pin's analog-safe limit even when the MCU is unpowered and a host is plugged in, and it has to stay above the threshold for a reliable logic high.

5 V across 47 kΩ and 82 kΩ Vsense=VbusRbRa+Rb=3.18 V Under the 3.6 V analog-safe ceiling with 420 mV to spare, and well over the 2.31 V needed for a logic high. Total 129 kΩ, so it draws 39 µA.
Why not ST's own recommended values

The application note suggests 33 kΩ over 82 kΩ, which gives 3.57 V. That clears the same 3.6 V ceiling by about 30 mV, which is inside the tolerance of the resistors themselves.

Shifting the top resistor to 47 kΩ moves it to 3.18 V and buys 420 mV of margin for the cost of nothing, since both values are standard and the current drops slightly too. The pin is five-volt tolerant in digital mode, so this margin is really protecting the option of reading it as an analog input later, where that tolerance doesn't apply.

06Running on bench power

USB 5 V and the buck's 5 V are the same rail, which raises an obvious problem: with a battery connected the buck would push current back into the host's port, and with USB connected the host would be feeding the battery side of the board.

A single Schottky diode fixes both. Anode on the USB side, cathode on the rail, so current only ever flows into the board.

about 0.3 V forward drop at bench current Vrail=Vbus−Vf≈4.7 V Leaves 1.4 V across the 3.3 V regulators, comfortably above their dropout, so the rails hold on USB alone.

A passive diode is the right choice here even though it wastes a few hundred millivolts. Active OR-ing controllers exist to recover that drop in multi-amp paths, and this port supplies well under an amp for configuration only. The drop is irrelevant to the budget and the part is one component with nothing to go wrong.
